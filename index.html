Why now

The attacker's side of the equation was industrialised

The argument for formal verification used to be made on principle: proof is better than testing because proof is complete. That argument was true but commercially weak, because in practice the adversary was also incomplete. Both sides sampled. Through 2024–2026 that symmetry broke — not through a novel technique or a new ATT&CK entry, but through a collapse in what it costs to try one.

4.5× the revenue of AI-assisted scam operations versus their non-AI equivalents Chainalysis, Jan 2026
USD 3.52 average compute cost per LLM-agent exploit attempt against real one-day CVEs Fang et al., arXiv 2404.08144
87% of a 15-CVE sample exploited by an LLM agent given only the public description same study · 0% for every non-GPT-4 agent tested

The cost of attempting an exploit has fallen to roughly the price of a coffee, the success rate is non-trivial, and the return on an automated campaign is several times the manual equivalent. That changes how many times the existing techniques get tried, and therefore how thin a control's coverage can afford to be.

Formal verification does not address the majority of 2025 dollars. The majority walked out through key management and operational security — compromised signers, mismanaged multisig quorums, off-boarding failures, social engineering of privileged operators. No amount of contract verification prevents a signer whose key an attacker holds, and anyone claiming otherwise is overselling. What verification addresses is narrower and more defensible: the class of failure that scales with protocol complexity rather than with operational discipline — roughly 89% of 2025 DeFi-protocol losses, and the bridge exploits that were about 73% of DeFi losses in 2022.

The honest scoping of the claim

AI is also the new attack surface

An enterprise LLM agent with tool-calling permissions is, architecturally, a remote code execution primitive wrapped in natural language. It is a non-human identity holding long-lived credentials, calling internal APIs, crossing trust boundaries, and deciding what to do next based on text that may include attacker-controlled content. It is the highest-privilege, least-governed identity class most enterprises have ever deployed, and it typically sits outside whatever PAM regime governs their human administrators.

OWASP LLM01

Prompt injection, direct and indirect

Instructions smuggled through retrieved documents, tool outputs or user data. Architecturally this is injection in the classical sense — control and data sharing a channel, exactly as in SQL injection, but with no parameterisation primitive available.

ATT&CK TA0004

Confused-deputy escalation

The agent holds broader standing privilege than the requesting user and can be manipulated into exercising it. Invisible to detection tuned on anomalous credentials, because the credential used is the correct one.

OWASP LLM06

Excessive agency and unsafe composition

Individually permitted tool calls composing into an unsafe trajectory that no single-call authorisation check rejects. The agent's effective permissions are the transitive closure of its tool graph, not the union of its individual grants.

Structural

Boundary erosion via context

Data-access limits enforced only by instructions in a system prompt, which is a request rather than an access control. A control the subject can argue with is not a control.

Guardrails implemented as another model judging the first model's output are probabilistic mitigations of a probabilistic failure — detective controls with a tunable false-negative rate, not preventive ones. A regulator asking “can this agent ever disclose a customer's PII?” is asking a question a classifier's confidence score cannot answer. And alongside what an agent does sits a separate question: whether what it says actually follows from the policy the organisation operates under. That is an entailment failure rather than an access-control failure, it lands on the second line rather than the SOC, and it needs different machinery.

The thesis in one line
The method

What automated reasoning actually does

We translate an artefact your estate already contains — an IAM policy set, a network topology, a contract, a protocol, a firmware build, an agent's tool manifest — into a precise logical model, state the security property as a formula, and ask a solver whether any assignment of variables violates it. The answer is one of exactly two things, and both are useful.

UNSAT

No such assignment exists

Not “none was found.” None exists, over the entire space, under the stated model. An adversary that enumerates finds what exists; if nothing exists within the modelled space, enumeration there finds nothing — and that is precisely what a proof establishes and a test cannot.

SAT + witness

Here is the exact violation

The request, network path, call ordering or input sequence that produces it — an attack path with a named entry condition, reproducible on your own infrastructure and ready to hand to the remediation owner. Not a severity score. The path.

Note the qualifier, because it is doing real work: within the modelled space. Everything outside the model — the asset nobody inventoried, the stack that drifted from its IaC template, the third party with a VPN account — remains exactly where vulnerability management, EDR, SIEM detections, threat hunting and red-teaming earn their keep. A proof is relative to a model and a specification, and every deliverable we issue states both.

This is already industrial practice

Formal verification stayed confined to microkernels, compilers and avionics for thirty years for a good reason: it was extraordinarily expensive. seL4's ~8,700 lines of C required on the order of 200,000 lines of Isabelle/HOL and about 20 person-years. What changed is that SMT solvers replaced interactive proof for large classes of problem — for the work below, most of the effort is not theorem-proving, it is faithful modelling. Most defenders already consume the result without knowing it.

AI Security Solutions

Solutions 6–7 · what an agent may do, and what it may say

Solution 6 bounds what an agent may do — its tool calls, its retrieval surface, the composition of its actions. That is an access-control problem, and it belongs to the CISO. Solution 7 bounds what it may say — whether an assertion the model makes actually follows from the policy that governs it. That is a model risk problem, and it belongs to the second line. Neither subsumes the other: an agent can stay perfectly inside its action envelope and still tell a customer something its own policy contradicts — no tool misused, no boundary crossed, no detection fired, and the output still wrong in a way that carries liability.

The free gap assessment is the standard entry point for enterprise security sales in the GCC, and it is a poor fit here: forty hours of interviews ending in a maturity heat map produces the artefact every competitor produces, assesses control design rather than control effectiveness, and demonstrates nothing distinctive. We start instead with a rapid, tool-driven analysis of one narrow, high-risk slice — a result about your own estate that you did not previously have. We call it Milestone 1 rather than a trial because serious clients are buying a programme with a known first step; nothing about it obliges anyone to commission Milestone 2.

  1. You scope it narrowly — one non-production cloud account or subscription. Read-only credentials are sufficient.

  3. You receive the counter-example — or the clearance. If a violation exists: the exact request, network path, call ordering or adversarial execution that reaches it, reproducible on your side. If none exists: a proof, with model assumptions and bounds stated.
